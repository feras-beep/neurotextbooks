# Status Epilepticus Management

## Overview

Life-threatening neurological emergency requiring rapid, tiered escalation of treatment. Average mortality 20% (up to 57% in some series). Over half of patients with SE do not have epilepsy.

**Sources**:
- The Walton Centre NHS Foundation Trust (Version 1.0, May 2020)
- Cambridge University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust NCCU (Version 3, December 2019)

---

## Definitions

### Status Epilepticus (SE)
Seizures lasting **>5 minutes** OR recurrent seizures without recovery of consciousness between episodes

**Note**: Traditional definition was >30 minutes, but revised to >5 minutes to prevent treatment delay

### Classification by Refractoriness

| Stage | Definition | Timing | Treatment |
|-------|-----------|--------|-----------|
| **Early SE** | Initial presentation | 0-30 min | Benzodiazepines |
| **Established SE** | Benzodiazepine-refractory | 30-60 min | IV anticonvulsants |
| **Refractory SE** | Fails 1st & 2nd line therapy | >60 min | General anaesthesia |
| **Super-Refractory SE** | Continues/recurs despite GA | >24 hours | Specialist therapies |

### Types

1. **Generalised Convulsive SE**: Rhythmic jerking of limbs with impaired consciousness (most common, highest morbidity/mortality)
2. **Non-Convulsive SE**: Seizure activity on EEG without clear convulsions (requires EEG diagnosis)
3. **Focal Aware Motor SE**: Localized motor seizures with retained consciousness

**This guideline focuses on generalised convulsive SE**

---

## Common Causes

### In Adults
- **Stroke** (vascular)
- **Hypoxic brain injury**
- **Metabolic derangements** (hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia, uraemia)
- **Alcohol** intoxication or withdrawal (most common in young adults)
- **AED non-compliance** (in known epilepsy)
- **CNS infection** (encephalitis, meningitis)
- **Tumours**
- **Drug toxicity**

### In Children
- **Infection** (febrile seizures, CNS infection)

**Important**: Patients with known epilepsy may have an acute precipitant for their SE

## Timeline-Based Management Algorithm

### Stage 0: Initial Management (0-5 minutes)

**ABCDE Approach**

**Airway & Breathing**
- Position patient to avoid injury (do NOT restrain)
- Airway adjunct if safe
- Oxygen to maintain SpO₂ >96%
- Semi-prone position, head down (prevent aspiration)

**Circulation**
- Establish IV access
- 0.9% sodium chloride for fluid resuscitation

**Disability**
- Check blood glucose: if hypoglycaemic give **150-200ml 10% glucose** IV stat
  - If continues fitting AND hypoglycaemic: repeat glucose bolus + start 10% glucose infusion at 100ml/hr
  - If alcohol excess/malnutrition suspected: **Pabrinex 1 pair IV BEFORE glucose**

**Exposure & Environment**
- Treat pyrexia aggressively

**Investigations** (do not delay treatment)
- **Bloods**: FBC, U&E (Na, K, Ca, Mg), glucose, LFTs, clotting, arterial blood gas
- **AED levels** if on regular anticonvulsants (phenytoin, carbamazepine, valproate, phenobarbital)
  - External lab for: lamotrigine, levetiracetam, lacosamide
- **Toxicology screen** if cause unknown
- **12-lead ECG**
- **CXR** (assess for aspiration)
- Save **5ml serum + 50ml urine** for potential future analysis

---

### Stage 1: First-Line Treatment (5-15 minutes)

**Benzodiazepines**

**If IV access available:**
- **Lorazepam 4mg IV** (reduce to 2mg in debilitated/elderly)
  - Wait 5 minutes
  - If ongoing: **repeat 4mg IV lorazepam**

**If NO IV access:**
- **Midazolam 10mg buccal** OR **10mg IM**
  - Wait 5 minutes
  - If ongoing: **repeat 10mg buccal/IM midazolam**

**Alternative (less preferred):**
- **Diazepam 10mg IV** (0.15-0.25mg/kg) as intravenous emulsion (Diazemuls®)
  - OR **10-20mg rectal** (Stesolid®)

**Key Points:**
- Up to **35-45% are benzodiazepine-refractory**
- Dose-dependent respiratory depression (monitor closely)
- Patients receiving ≥3mg/kg diazepam (or equivalent) in <24 hours likely need prolonged ventilatory support

---

### Stage 2: Second-Line Treatment (15-60 minutes)

**Established SE = Benzodiazepine-refractory SE**

**Ensure IV/IO access** and **alert anaesthetist**

**Choose ONE of the following anticonvulsants:**

#### Levetiracetam (FIRST-LINE)
- **Loading**: 60mg/kg IV (maximum 4500mg) in 100ml 0.9% NaCl over 10 minutes
- **Maintenance**: Start ≥4 hours after loading
  - 1000-1500mg IV/NG/PO twice daily (BD)
  - Adjust for renal impairment (eGFR <50ml/min)

**Advantages:**
- Fewest drug interactions
- No cardiac monitoring required
- Well tolerated

**Avoid if:**
- Mood or behavioural disorder (may worsen symptoms)

#### Phenytoin (SECOND-LINE)
- **Loading**: 20mg/kg IV (maximum 2000mg) at **50mg/min** rate
  - Reduce to **25mg/min** in elderly or cardiac disease
  - Give **undiluted** via **large bore IV** (risk of extravasation/phlebitis)
  - Round down to nearest 250mg
- **Maintenance**: Start ≥6 hours after loading
  - 100mg IV TDS initially, adjust to levels
  - Convert to oral once stable (150-300mg OD at night)

**Requirements:**
- **Continuous cardiac monitoring** (ECG + BP)
- Wide bore IV access

**Avoid if:**
- No cardiac monitoring available
- Known/suspected generalised epilepsy (genetic epilepsy)
- Hypotension/bradycardia/heart block
- Porphyria
- Recreational drug overdose/alcohol withdrawal

**Important:**
- 90% protein-bound: correct levels if hypoalbuminaemia
- Enteral phenytoin: **hold feeds 2 hours before and after dose**
- See also: [[Phenytoin in Adults Guideline]]

#### Sodium Valproate (THIRD-LINE)
- **Loading**: 40mg/kg IV (maximum 3000mg) in 100ml 0.9% NaCl over 5 minutes
- **Maintenance**: Start ≥6 hours after loading
  - 10mg/kg/day divided BD-TDS (typical: 1000mg BD)
  - Maximum 2.5g daily for maintenance

**Preferred if:**
- Known/suspected idiopathic generalised epilepsy
- Co-morbid mood disorder/migraines

**AVOID if:**
- **Women of childbearing potential** (teratogenic - 30-40% neurodevelopmental risk, 10% congenital malformation risk)
  - Consider pregnancy test before use
- Active liver disease
- Pancreatitis
- Mitochondrial disease/metabolic disorder
- Porphyria

**Monitoring:**
- LFTs, FBC, ammonia (risk of hyperammonaemic encephalopathy)

---

### ESETT Trial (2019)
**Landmark Study**: Established Status Epilepticus Treatment Trial

**Conclusion**: No significant difference in efficacy or adverse events between **levetiracetam, phenytoin (fosphenytoin), and valproate** for established SE

**Implication**: Drug choice should be individualized based on:
- Patient factors (age, sex, comorbidities)
- Seizure type (focal vs generalized)
- Drug interactions
- Contraindications

---

### Maintenance Dosing After Loading

**Timing for first maintenance dose:**
- **Levetiracetam**: 4-14 hours after loading (allows BD dosing schedule)
- **Phenytoin**: 6-8 hours after loading
- **Valproate**: 6-14 hours after loading

**Important**: Continue patient's regular AEDs if already prescribed

---

### Stage 3: Third-Line Treatment (>60 minutes)

**Refractory SE = Failure of benzodiazepine + adequate anticonvulsant**

**Criteria for ICU Admission:**
- SE fails to terminate after 1st and 2nd line therapy
- Respiratory depression requiring airway support
- Depressed conscious level with ongoing electrographic seizures

**Management:**

1. **Intubation and mechanical ventilation**
2. **Continuous EEG monitoring** (MANDATORY)
   - To detect non-convulsive SE (after convulsions cease)
   - To titrate anaesthetic agents
   - BIS monitor insufficient (low quality, misses focal seizures)
3. **General anaesthesia with continuous infusion**
4. **Start/optimize maintenance anticonvulsant** during GA

**Treatment Goal:**
- **Burst suppression on EEG** OR
- **Complete seizure suppression**

**Duration:**
- Continue GA for minimum **24 hours**
- Then attempt **gradual taper** (4-8 hours) under continuous EEG

## Third-Line Agents: General Anaesthesia

### Propofol

| **Loading** | **Maintenance** | **Maximum** |
|------------|----------------|------------|
| 1-2mg/kg bolus over 10-20 seconds | Initial: 1-5mg/kg/hr | **4mg/kg/hr** |
| (Lower dose/slower rate if >55yo) | Titrate: increase by 0.2mg/kg/hr every 2 hours | (>5mg/kg/hr for max 3-4 hours only) |
| | Breakthrough: 1mg/kg bolus | |

**Advantages:**
- Rapid onset
- Short half-life (easy to wake up)

**Disadvantages:**
- **Propofol Infusion Syndrome (PRIS)** - life-threatening
  - Risk factors: dose >5mg/kg/hr, duration >48 hours
  - Features: metabolic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalaemia, renal failure, hypertriglyceridaemia, cardiac failure/arrest
  - ECG: **Brugada pattern** (downslope ST elevation in V1-V2) - reduce dose immediately

**Monitoring:**
- Daily: **ECG, triglycerides, creatine kinase, lactate**
- Lipid profile if >3 days
- **Liaise with dietician** (adjust lipid content of nutrition)

**Contraindications:**
- Propofol allergy
- Egg/soy allergy

**Notes:**
- In practice, efficacy limited by dose restrictions
- Safe doses may be insufficient to terminate EEG seizures

---

### Midazolam

| **Loading** | **Maintenance** | **Maximum** |
|------------|----------------|------------|
| 0.03-0.3mg/kg at 1mg/30sec | Initial: 0.03-0.1mg/kg/hr | 20mg/hr |
| (e.g. 80kg patient: max 24mg over 12 min) | Titrate: increase by 0.1mg/kg/hr every 4 hours | (higher on consultant advice) |
| | Breakthrough: 0.1-0.2mg/kg bolus | |

**Advantages:**
- Short-acting
- Familiar to intensivists

**Disadvantages:**
- **Tachyphylaxis** (larger doses needed with prolonged use)
- Accumulation in obese patients and renal insufficiency
- Hypotension (30-50% require vasopressors)
- Withdrawal syndrome
- **More breakthrough seizures** vs other agents

**Drug Interactions:**
- When used with phenytoin: may displace phenytoin from albumin (interpret levels cautiously)
- Multiple CYP3A4 interactions (see drug monograph)

---

### Thiopental (Thiopentone)

| **Loading** | **Maintenance** | **Maximum** |
|------------|----------------|------------|
| 75-125mg (or 3-5mg/kg) over 10-15 sec | Initial: 3mg/kg/hr | 800mg/hr |
| | Titrate: increase by 0.5mg/kg/hr every 6 hours | |
| | Breakthrough: 1-2mg/kg bolus | |

**Advantages:**
- Good efficacy
- Lowers body temperature (may be beneficial)

**Disadvantages:**
- **Very large volume of distribution** - accumulates, takes **days to wear off**
- Prolonged ventilator dependency
- Hypotension and cardiorespiratory depression (require vasopressors)
- **Hypokalaemia** during infusion
- **Rebound hyperkalaemia** on cessation (monitor K+ for 72 hours post-cessation)
- Zero-order kinetics at high doses (>35mg/l)

**Monitoring:**
- Continuous cardiac monitoring
- Potassium levels (during infusion, weaning, and 72 hours post-cessation)
- Plasma levels if doses >4mg/kg/hr
- LFTs, CK, FBC, CRP, temperature

**Expected Effects:**
- <200mcg/ml: spontaneous breathing expected
- <100mcg/ml: awakening expected

**Contraindications:**
- Porphyria

---

### Ketamine

| **Loading** | **Maintenance** | **Maximum** |
|------------|----------------|------------|
| 1mg/kg (range 0.4-4.5mg/kg) | Initial: 2mg/kg/hr | 10mg/kg/hr |
| | Titrate: range 0.3-7.5mg/kg/hr | |

**Mechanism**: NMDA receptor antagonist (different from GABAergic agents)

**Advantages:**
- **Minimal respiratory depression**
- **Minimal hypotension** (rare need for vasopressors)
- Short half-life (no toxic accumulation)
- Theoretical benefit in super-refractory SE (NMDA upregulation in prolonged SE)

**Disadvantages:**
- Hypertension (may increase ICP)
- Psychiatric effects (hallucinations, agitation)
- Limited evidence base (case series only)

**Monitoring:**
- Continuous cardiac and respiratory monitoring
- **BIS unreliable** with ketamine

**Contraindications:**
- Severe coronary/myocardial disease
- Cerebrovascular accident
- Conditions where elevated ICP may be detrimental

**Drug Interactions:**
- May potentiate atracurium (neuromuscular blockade)
- May antagonize thiopental (hypnotic effect)
- Diazepam competitively inhibits ketamine metabolism (prolonged effect)

**Note**: Increasing evidence supporting ketamine as alternative third-line agent, especially in refractory cases

## Critical Care Management Points

### EEG Monitoring
- **Continuous EEG monitoring MANDATORY** for:
  - All patients with depressed consciousness during/after SE
  - All patients on GA for SE
- **Goals**:
  - Detect non-convulsive SE (clinical convulsions may stop but EEG seizures continue)
  - Titrate anaesthetic depth to burst suppression or seizure suppression
- **Reduced montage (8-channel) EEG is sufficient**
- **BIS monitoring limitations**:
  - Low quality (1-2 channel raw EEG only)
  - Misses focal seizures
  - Artifact recognition difficult
  - Unreliable with ketamine
  - Should NOT replace conventional EEG

### Neuromuscular Blockade
- **AVOID if possible** (masks clinical seizure activity)
- If required: **continuous EEG monitoring essential**
- Use train-of-four monitoring

### Maintenance Anticonvulsants During GA
**Start maintenance AED therapy early** (don't wait for GA wean)

**First-line**: Levetiracetam 1000-1500mg IV/NG/PO BD

**Second-line**: Phenytoin 100mg IV TDS → convert to oral OD when stable

**Third-line**: Valproate 1000mg IV BD (caution in women of childbearing potential)

**If recurrence on first wean attempt**: Consider **phenobarbital bridging**
- Dose: 15mg/kg loading, then 60-180mg OD (at night)
- Caution: respiratory depression, prolonged sedation

---

### Stage 4: Super-Refractory SE (>24 hours)

**Definition**: SE continues or recurs **>24 hours** after GA initiation despite adequate treatment

**Most common cause**: Autoimmune encephalitis (especially NORSE = New Onset Refractory Status Epilepticus)

**Putative mechanism**: GABA receptor downregulation + glutamate (NMDA) receptor upregulation

---

## Management of Super-Refractory SE

### Step 1: Optimize Current Therapy
1. **Repeat GA cycle** (48 hours) with **alternative agent** from propofol/midazolam/thiopental
2. **Add additional anticonvulsant**:
   - **Lacosamide 400mg IV/NG loading** → 200mg BD maintenance
   - Requires continuous ECG monitoring (risk of cardiac conduction abnormalities)
3. **Ensure two AEDs of different mechanisms** are on board

### Step 2: Immunotherapy (if autoimmune suspected)
- **Pulse IV methylprednisolone 1g daily x 3 days**
- If no response after 2 days steroids, consider:
  - **IVIG** (intravenous immunoglobulin)
  - **Plasma exchange** (therapeutic plasmapheresis)
- Discuss with neurology for further immunomodulation

### Step 3: Adjunctive Therapies
1. **Hypothermia** (32-35°C) for <48 hours
   - ⚠️ **Warning**: If using propofol, switch to alternative agent before inducing hypothermia
2. **Magnesium infusion**
   - Loading: 20mmol IV magnesium sulphate
   - Infusion: 10-30mmol/hr (typical: 10mmol/hr)
   - Target serum magnesium: 1.5mmol/l
3. **Pyridoxine** 200mg/day NG/PO (if <18 years old)

### Step 4: Refractory Measures
1. **Ketogenic diet** (4:1 ratio)
   - ⚠️ **Warning**: Do NOT use with propofol
   - Liaise with dietician
2. **Neurosurgical resection** (if lesional SE identified on imaging)
3. **Vagal nerve stimulation (VNS)** - up to 1.25mA

### Step 5: Last Resort Therapies
- **CSF drainage** (if hydrocephalus/raised ICP)
- **Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)** - liaise with neurology

## Common Treatment Errors

### 1. Inadequate EEG Suppression
**Problem**: SE was never electroencephalographically controlled despite cessation of clinical convulsions

**Solution**: Continuous EEG monitoring; titrate GA to burst suppression or complete EEG seizure suppression

### 2. No Maintenance Anticonvulsant
**Problem**: SE controlled with GA, but no/inadequate maintenance AED initiated before wean

**Solution**: Start maintenance AED early during GA (levetiracetam, phenytoin, or valproate)

### 3. Too-Rapid GA Wean
**Problem**: SE controlled, but GA withdrawn too quickly → recurrence

**Solution**: Gradual taper over 4-8 hours under continuous EEG monitoring

### 4. Wrong Diagnosis
**Pseudo-refractory SE** may actually be:
- **Non-epileptic attacks** (psychogenic)
- **Paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity** (autonomic storming) - see [[PSH/PAID Syndrome]]

### 5. Inadequate Drug Dosing
- Failure to give full loading doses
- Not escalating GA to adequate EEG burst suppression

## Specific Clinical Scenarios

### Post-Traumatic Seizure Prophylaxis
**Indications for prophylaxis:**
- Depressed skull fracture
- Penetrating head injury
- Early post-traumatic seizure (<7 days)
- Intracerebral haematoma
- Severe TBI (GCS ≤8)

**Drug of choice**:
- **Levetiracetam** (preferred over phenytoin)
  - Meta-analysis: equivalent efficacy, better tolerability
  - No drug interactions
  - No need for level monitoring
- Duration: typically **7 days**

**Not routinely recommended** for all TBI patients

## Prognostication

### Poor Prognostic Factors
**Clinical:**
- Prolonged duration (>60 minutes)
- Refractory/super-refractory SE
- Need for multiple GA trials
- Older age
- Myoclonic status post-cardiac arrest

**Aetiological:**
- Anoxic brain injury
- Acute stroke
- CNS infection (encephalitis)
- Tumour

**Physiological:**
- Prolonged sedation requirement
- Inability to wean from GA

**Imaging:**
- Catastrophic/irreversible brain injury on MRI
- Note: High cortical signal can be reversible seizure effect

**Important Notes:**
- **No defined endpoint**: Continue treatment as long as no devastating brain injury visible
- **Good recovery possible** after months of prolonged/intermittent SE
- Consider underlying aetiology (e.g. autoimmune encephalitis may be treatable)

## Special Considerations

### Women of Childbearing Potential
- **Valproate**: Highly teratogenic (30-40% neurodevelopmental disorders, 10% congenital malformations)
  - Avoid if possible
  - If essential: pregnancy test, counsel on risks, implement pregnancy prevention programme
  - See: MHRA Valproate Pregnancy Prevention Programme
- **Phenytoin**: Teratogenic (lower risk than valproate)
- **Levetiracetam**: Safest option in pregnancy

### Drug Interactions
**Phenytoin** (extensive interactions):
- Enzyme inducer: reduces levels of many drugs
- 90% protein-bound: displaced by valproate, aspirin
- See drug monograph for full list

**Valproate**:
- Enzyme inhibitor: increases levels of many drugs
- Increases lamotrigine, phenobarbital, phenytoin levels

**Levetiracetam**:
- Fewest interactions (not hepatically metabolized)
- Reduced efficacy when given with macrogols (Movicol®, Laxido®) via enteral route
  - Separate doses by 1 hour

## Interview Key Points

### Management Principles
1. **Time-dependent escalation**: Don't delay treatment at any stage
2. **Benzodiazepines first** (lorazepam or midazolam)
3. **IV anticonvulsant second** (levetiracetam = first choice for most)
4. **GA third** if >60 minutes despite adequate treatment
5. **Continuous EEG monitoring** for all patients with depressed consciousness
6. **Start maintenance AEDs early** during GA
7. **Gradual GA wean** (4-8 hours) under EEG monitoring
8. **Identify and treat underlying cause** (infection, metabolic, structural, autoimmune)

### Drug Selection Strategy

**Second-line anticonvulsant selection:**

| Clinical Scenario | Preferred Drug | Avoid |
|-------------------|---------------|-------|
| Unknown seizure type | **Levetiracetam** | - |
| Known focal epilepsy | Levetiracetam or Phenytoin | - |
| Known generalised epilepsy | **Valproate** | Phenytoin |
| Polypharmacy | **Levetiracetam** (fewest interactions) | - |
| Woman of childbearing age | **Levetiracetam** | Valproate |
| Cardiac disease | Levetiracetam or Valproate | Phenytoin |
| Liver disease | Levetiracetam or Phenytoin | Valproate |
| Alcohol withdrawal | Levetiracetam or Phenytoin | Phenytoin (if overdose) |
| Mood disorder | Phenytoin or Valproate | Levetiracetam |

## Common Interview Questions

**Q: Define status epilepticus.**
A: Seizures lasting >5 minutes OR recurrent seizures without recovery of consciousness between episodes. Revised from traditional >30 minutes definition to prevent treatment delay.

**Q: What are the stages of status epilepticus and how are they treated?**
A:
- **Early SE** (0-30 min): Benzodiazepines (lorazepam 4mg IV or midazolam 10mg buccal/IM)
- **Established SE** (30-60 min): IV anticonvulsants (levetiracetam 60mg/kg, phenytoin 20mg/kg, or valproate 40mg/kg)
- **Refractory SE** (>60 min): General anaesthesia with continuous infusion (propofol, midazolam, thiopental, or ketamine)
- **Super-refractory SE** (>24 hours): Specialist therapies (immunotherapy, hypothermia, ketogenic diet, neurosurgery)

**Q: Why is levetiracetam preferred as second-line treatment?**
A:
- Equal efficacy to phenytoin and valproate (ESETT trial)
- Fewest drug interactions (not hepatically metabolized)
- No need for cardiac monitoring
- Safe in cardiac disease, pregnancy, liver disease
- Can be given rapidly (10 minutes)
- Well tolerated

**Q: What are the complications of propofol in status epilepticus?**
A: **Propofol infusion syndrome (PRIS)** - potentially fatal:
- Features: metabolic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalaemia, renal failure, hypertriglyceridaemia, cardiac dysfunction
- Risk factors: dose >5mg/kg/hr, duration >48 hours
- ECG: Brugada pattern (downsloping ST elevation V1-V2)
- Prevention: limit dose to 4mg/kg/hr; monitor daily ECG, CK, lactate, triglycerides
- Management: stop propofol, supportive care

**Q: When would you use ketamine for status epilepticus?**
A: Consider ketamine as third-line agent:
- Super-refractory SE (different mechanism - NMDA antagonist)
- When propofol/midazolam/thiopental ineffective or contraindicated
- Theoretical benefit: addresses NMDA receptor upregulation in prolonged SE
- Advantages: minimal respiratory depression and hypotension
- Caution: may increase ICP, avoid in severe cardiac disease

**Q: What is the role of continuous EEG monitoring?**
A: **Essential for:**
- Detecting non-convulsive SE after clinical convulsions cease
- Titrating GA to burst suppression/seizure suppression
- Monitoring during GA wean
- Differentiating SE from non-epileptic attacks

**Minimum requirement**: Reduced montage (8-channel) EEG
**Not adequate**: BIS monitor (low quality, misses focal seizures)

**Q: What are the common causes of pseudo-refractory status epilepticus?**
A:
1. **Inadequate drug dosage** - failure to suppress EEG seizures
2. **Wrong diagnosis** - non-epileptic attacks or paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity
3. **Missed underlying cause** - ongoing infection, metabolic derangement, structural lesion

**Q: When would you consider immunotherapy?**
A: In **super-refractory SE**, especially:
- **NORSE** (new onset refractory status epilepticus) - no prior epilepsy history
- Suspected autoimmune encephalitis
- Young patient without clear structural cause
- No response to standard therapies

**Treatment**: IV methylprednisolone 1g daily x 3 days → IVIG or plasma exchange if no response

**Q: What maintenance anticonvulsants should be started during general anaesthesia?**
A: Start early (don't wait for wean):
- **First-line**: Levetiracetam 1000-1500mg BD (IV/NG/PO)
- **Second-line**: Phenytoin 100mg TDS IV → convert to oral OD
- **Third-line**: Valproate 1000mg BD (avoid in women of childbearing age)
- **Consider two AEDs** of different mechanisms
- **Phenobarbital bridging** if first wean unsuccessful

**Q: How do you wean general anaesthesia?**
A:
- Wait minimum **24 hours** after achieving EEG burst suppression
- **Gradual taper** over 4-8 hours
- **Continuous EEG monitoring** during wean
- If seizures recur: re-institute GA for another 48 hours with alternative agent
- Ensure adequate maintenance AEDs before attempting wean

**Q: What are the contraindications to sodium valproate?**
A:
- **Absolute**: Women of childbearing potential (unless pregnancy prevention programme), active liver disease, pancreatitis, mitochondrial disease, porphyria
- **Relative**: Risk of hyperammonaemic encephalopathy (especially with topiramate)
- **Teratogenicity**: 30-40% neurodevelopmental disorders, 10% congenital malformations

**Q: Why avoid neuromuscular blockade in status epilepticus?**
A:
- Masks clinical seizure activity (can't assess treatment response)
- EEG seizures may continue despite clinical paralysis
- If essential: **continuous EEG monitoring mandatory**
- Use train-of-four to monitor depth

**Q: What are the causes of super-refractory status epilepticus?**
A: Most common: **Autoimmune encephalitis**
- NORSE (new onset refractory status epilepticus)
- Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis
- Other autoimmune encephalitides (anti-LGI1, anti-CASPR2, etc.)

Other causes: CNS infection, paraneoplastic, cryptogenic

**Mechanism**: GABA receptor downregulation + NMDA receptor upregulation

**Q: What is the role of phenobarbital?**
A:
- **Second-line alternative** if levetiracetam/phenytoin/valproate all contraindicated
- **Bridging agent** if first GA wean unsuccessful
  - Dose: 15mg/kg loading, then 60-180mg OD (at night)
  - Levels <200mcg/ml: spontaneous breathing; <100mcg/ml: awakening
- **Disadvantages**: Respiratory depression, prolonged sedation, enzyme inducer

## Key Takeaways for Viva

1. **Don't wait**: SE is defined as >5 minutes to prompt early treatment
2. **Tiered approach**: Benzodiazepines → IV anticonvulsant → GA
3. **Levetiracetam first**: Unless specific indication for phenytoin or valproate
4. **EEG monitoring essential**: For all patients with depressed consciousness
5. **Start maintenance AEDs early**: During GA, not after wean
6. **Propofol dose limit**: 4mg/kg/hr to avoid PRIS
7. **Consider immunotherapy**: In super-refractory SE (especially NORSE)
8. **Two common mistakes**: Inadequate EEG suppression and no maintenance AEDs
