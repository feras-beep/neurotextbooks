# Case 03 - Vestibular Schwannoma Management

## Case Presentation

**Patient:** 51-year-old woman

**History:**
- Previously seen in clinic with decreased hearing on left, tinnitus and unsteadiness
- One-year follow-up scan shows **increase in size** of lesion (now 2cm in diameter)
- Complains of worsening headaches

**Imaging:**
- T1-weighted axial and coronal MRI images
- Discrete contrast-enhancing lesion at cerebello-pontine angle
- Cystic central component
- Intracanalicular component present

---

## Questions & Model Answers

### 1. Describe the images shown, what is the most likely diagnosis?

## Imaging Interpretation

**Sequence:** T1-weighted axial (top row) and coronal MRI images

**Findings:**
- **Discrete contrast-enhancing lesion** (top right) at the **cerebello-pontine angle**
- **Cystic central component**
- **Intracanalicular component** present

**Most Likely Diagnosis:** **Vestibular schwannoma** (or acoustic neuroma)

### Radiological Features Typical of Vestibular Schwannoma

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Location** | Cerebello-pontine angle (CPA) |
| **Origin** | Internal auditory meatus/canal |
| **Enhancement** | Avid contrast enhancement |
| **Shape** | "Ice cream cone" - widening of IAM + CPA component |
| **T1** | Isointense to hypointense |
| **T2** | Hyperintense |
| **Cystic change** | Common in larger tumors |

---

### 2. From where do these tumours arise?

## Anatomical Origin

**Most tumours arise from:**
- The **Obersteiner-Redlich zone**
- Approximately **1cm from where the nerve exits the brainstem**

**Nerve of Origin:**
- The most commonly affected nerve is the **superior division of the vestibular nerve**
- Hence the term **vestibular schwannoma** is favoured over acoustic neuroma

### Anatomy of Vestibulocochlear Nerve (CN VIII)

The vestibulocochlear nerve has **two components**:

1. **Vestibular division** (balance):
   - Superior vestibular nerve
   - Inferior vestibular nerve

2. **Cochlear division** (hearing)

**Obersteiner-Redlich Zone:**
- Transition between **central myelin** (oligodendrocytes) and **peripheral myelin** (Schwann cells)
- Most vulnerable point for schwannoma development

---

### 3. Which presenting signs and symptoms are the most common?

## Clinical Presentation

### Cardinal Feature
**Progressive unilateral (unless lesions are bilateral) hearing loss** is the **cardinal feature** and is present in **at least 95% of cases**.

### Common Symptoms (in order of frequency)

| Symptom | Frequency | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| **Hearing loss** | 95%+ | Progressive, unilateral, sensorineural |
| **Tinnitus** | >90% | Unilateral, high-pitched |
| **Vertigo/Disequilibrium** | 60-70% | Often early symptom |
| **Headache** | 30-50% | Occipital, worse with large tumors |
| **Otalgia** | Less common | Ear pain |
| **Facial numbness** | Less common | CN V involvement with larger tumors |
| **Facial weakness** | Rare | Late sign; poor prognostic factor |

### Clinical Examination Findings

**Audiometry:**
- **High-frequency sensorineural hearing loss** is the most common result
- May progress to complete deafness

**Rinne's Test:**
- **Positive** (air conduction > bone conduction)

**Weber's Test:**
- Patient would **localise sound to the unaffected side**

**Other Findings:**
- Reduced corneal reflex (CN V)
- Facial nerve dysfunction (rare, late)
- Cerebellar signs (ataxia, nystagmus)
- Signs of raised ICP if large tumor

---

### 4. What other tests might be requested prior to treatment?

## Pre-Treatment Investigations

### Audiological Assessment

**Pure tone audiogram** may be performed in order to:
- **Document** hearing loss
- **Monitor** hearing loss over time

**Importance:**
Although this is **not essential**, high-frequency hearing loss is the **most common result** for patients with a vestibular schwannoma.

### Additional Tests

| Investigation | Purpose |
|---------------|---------|
| **Speech discrimination** | Assess functional hearing |
| **Brainstem auditory evoked responses (BAER)** | Assess neural pathway function |
| **Vestibular function tests** | Caloric testing, VNG |
| **MRI IAM protocol** | High-resolution imaging for surgical planning |
| **Facial nerve function assessment** | Baseline for comparison |

---

### 5. What treatment options are available?

## Management Strategies

The management of these lesions can be divided into: **expectant**, **stereotactic radiosurgery**, and **surgical**.

### 1. Expectant Management (Conservative/Wait and Scan)

**Indications:**
- Relatively **rapid tumour growth** (most lesions grow at a rate of **<10mm/year**)
- **Some symptom progression**

**Strategy:**
- Expectant management is probably **inappropriate** unless the patient refuses treatment
- This patient has shown relatively rapid tumor growth (most lesions grow <10mm/year) and symptom progression

**Monitoring:**
- Serial MRI scans (typically annually)
- Audiometry

---

### 2. Stereotactic Radiosurgery

**Indications:**
- Lesions with **maximal diameter <3cm**
- Usually also a policy on the **maximum number of lesions** that can be treated
- In Leeds, patients must have **≤3 lesions**

**Technique:**
- See [[Gamma Knife - Principles and Applications]]

**Outcomes:**
- **Hearing preservation**: Quoted at **50%** in some series
- **Post-operative complications** (facial numbness, weakness, tumor swelling): Incidence low
- **Long-term follow-up** data (recurrence rates) are **unknown**

**Advantages:**
- Non-invasive
- Outpatient procedure
- Good tumor control rates

**Disadvantages:**
- Tumor remains in situ
- Risk of delayed complications
- Cannot obtain tissue diagnosis
- Long-term efficacy unknown

---

### 3. Surgery

**Indications:**
- Patient preference
- Rapidly growing tumor
- Large tumors (>3cm)
- Young patients (longer life expectancy)

**Potential Benefits:**
- **Complete tumour resection** possible
- **Up to 97% of cases** if this can be achieved
- **Recurrence rates** may be as low as **5% over a 10-year follow-up period**

**Surgical Approaches:** See Question 2 (Case 14) for detailed comparison

---

## Surgical Approaches - Summary

### Three Classical Approaches

| Approach | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|----------|------------|---------------|
| **Retrosigmoid (Suboccipital)** | • Highest chance of hearing preservation<br>• Access to cerebellopontine angle<br>• Can be used for any size tumor | • Late visualisation of facial nerve<br>• Higher post-operative morbidity<br>• Risk of CSF leak |
| **Translabyrinthine** | • Early visualisation of facial nerve<br>• Useful for large intracanalicular component<br>• Lower risk CSF leak | • Sacrifices hearing<br>• Difficult to excise larger lesions<br>• Increased surgical duration<br>• Poor access to CP angle |
| **Middle Fossa (Extradural Subtemporal)** | • Potential for hearing preservation in small intracanalicular lesions<br>• Early facial nerve identification | • Only suitable for small, laterally-located lesions<br>• Increased facial nerve damage<br>• Temporal lobe retraction |

### Modern Combined Approaches

- **Translabyrinthine + retrosigmoid** can be combined in some cases
- **Stereotactic radiosurgery** for residuum post-operatively

## Histopathology Features

### Macroscopic Appearance
- Well-circumscribed
- Encapsulated
- Yellow/tan color
- May have cystic areas
- Attached to nerve

### Microscopic Features

**Classic patterns:**

1. **Antoni A areas:**
   - Densely cellular
   - Spindle cells
   - Verocay bodies (nuclear palisading)

2. **Antoni B areas:**
   - Loosely arranged
   - Myxoid background
   - Microcystic change

**Immunohistochemistry:**
- **S100 positive** (diagnostic)
- **GFAP negative** (distinguishes from astrocytoma)

**WHO Grading:** Grade I (benign)

---

## Special Considerations

### Neurofibromatosis Type 2 (NF2)

**Key Features:**
- **Bilateral** vestibular schwannomas (diagnostic)
- Autosomal dominant
- Chromosome 22q12 mutation (NF2 gene - codes for merlin protein)
- Multiple meningiomas, ependymomas, schwannomas

**Manchester Criteria for NF2:**

Bilateral VS **OR**

Family history + unilateral VS <30 years **OR**

Family history + **two of:**
- Meningioma
- Glioma
- Schwannoma
- Posterior subcapsular lenticular opacities (cataracts)

### Hearing Preservation

**Factors affecting hearing preservation:**
- **Pre-operative hearing** (most important)
- **Tumor size** (<1.5cm better)
- **Surgical approach** (middle fossa or retrosigmoid better than translabyrinthine)
- **Surgical technique** (avoid cochlear nerve injury, preserve vascular supply)
- **Anatomical preservation** of cochlear nerve

**Grading Scales:**
- **Gardner-Robertson scale** (Classes I-V)
- **AAO-HNS hearing classification**

---

## Complications of Surgery

### Intra-operative
- Hemorrhage
- CSF leak
- Cranial nerve injury
- Brainstem injury
- Cerebellar injury

### Early Post-operative
- **Facial nerve dysfunction** (most common)
- Hearing loss
- CSF leak (10-15%)
- Meningitis
- Hemorrhage
- Hydrocephalus

### Late Post-operative
- **Facial numbness**
- **Weakness**
- **Tumour swelling** (pseudoprogression)
- Recurrence
- Balance problems

### Facial Nerve Outcomes

**House-Brackmann Scale** for grading facial nerve function:

| Grade | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **I** | Normal function |
| **II** | Mild dysfunction (slight weakness, normal at rest) |
| **III** | Moderate dysfunction (obvious asymmetry, complete eye closure) |
| **IV** | Moderately severe (obvious weakness, incomplete eye closure) |
| **V** | Severe dysfunction (barely perceptible motion) |
| **VI** | Complete paralysis |

## Common Interview Questions

**Q: What is the natural history of vestibular schwannomas?**

A: Variable growth patterns:
- **30-50%** remain stable in size
- **40-60%** grow slowly (1-2mm/year average)
- **10-15%** grow rapidly (>5mm/year)
- Rapid growth more common in younger patients
- Cystic tumors may grow faster

**Q: What is the differential diagnosis of a CPA lesion?**

A:
1. **Vestibular schwannoma** (80-90%)
2. **Meningioma** (5-10%) - dural tail, broader base
3. **Epidermoid cyst** - bright on DWI, "pearly" appearance
4. **Arachnoid cyst** - follows CSF signal on all sequences
5. **Metastasis** - usually multiple lesions
6. **Trigeminal schwannoma** - different location
7. **Glomus jugulare** - salt and pepper, flow voids

**Q: How do you differentiate vestibular schwannoma from meningioma on imaging?**

| Feature | Vestibular Schwannoma | Meningioma |
|---------|----------------------|------------|
| **Origin** | IAM (ice cream cone) | Dural surface |
| **Enhancement** | Homogeneous | Homogeneous + dural tail |
| **Shape** | Rounded | Broadly based |
| **Cysts** | Common | Less common |
| **Calcification** | Rare | Common |
